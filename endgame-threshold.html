Endgame threshold

Fourteen pieces, seven a side, and no pawns; a click takes any piece off the board and puts it back. Pawns never leave, so counting them only muddies the question.

The points rule is Speelman's: an endgame is a position where each player holds thirteen points or less of material, kings excluded, using the ordinary nine for a queen, five for a rook, three for a bishop or knight. The ratio rule counts pieces instead of points. Seven trade units a side, and the endgame arrives once four have gone and three are left, a ratio of 4 to 3.

The two rules disagree twice. With every minor piece traded, the ratio rule calls it an endgame at 4 to 3, while nineteen points of queen and rooks sit on the board waiting to attack. With the queen and both rooks traded instead, the ratio rule still says middlegame at 3 to 4, though four minor pieces and pawns play like an endgame by anyone's account. Composition beats the count. Weighting the units to fix this rebuilds the points rule.

Reaching the threshold

Thirteen points remaining means eighteen or more points traded, out of thirty one. Exactly eighteen has one solution, queen plus three minors. Everything at or past the line:

Twelve ways in, and m stands for a minor piece. Two totals repeat, marked in darker type. At nineteen points traded and again at twenty two, two different sets of pieces carry identical value, and both reduce to a queen facing three minor pieces. Every other total is unique, so unequal totals mean one side is simply ahead rather than holding a different kind of army.

Totals of 24, 27, 29 and 30 cannot be built from nines, fives and threes under these constraints, so the scale has gaps near the top.

What the engines count

Engines gave up on thresholds. Tapered evaluation computes a phase value from the non-pawn material left standing, weighting a knight or bishop 1, a rook 2, and a queen 4, for 24 at the start, then blends a middlegame score with an endgame score in that proportion. Nothing flips. The idea appeared in Berliner's work in 1979 and in engines like The King and Phalanx, and spread widely after Fruit. The third readout above shows it, reporting the game thirty percent of the way to an ending while both other rules still say middlegame.

Other people's lines

  • Alekhine held that the boundary cannot be stated at all.
  • Fine: no queens on the board.
  • Minev: four or fewer pieces besides kings and pawns.
  • Flear: at most one piece each besides kings and pawns.
  • Speelman: thirteen points or less each, or alternatively, the point at which the king becomes usable as an attacking piece.

The points rule has a known flaw worth sitting with. Because it looks only at material, a position can leave the endgame and return to it, and a king, queen, rook and pawn against king, queen and rook can follow a rook and pawn ending. Phases were never guaranteed to run in order.
