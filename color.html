Color

Color is not in the light. Light has a wavelength; color is what a brain makes of the answers of three kinds of cone, tuned to short, middle and long waves, and every hue anyone has seen is a mixture of three numbers. The first view is those three curves, with the rod that works at night, over the spectrum; the marker reads how each answers to any wavelength.

The second view is the map of everything the eye can see, the horseshoe of the 1931 diagram, with the small triangle inside it that a screen can show, the curve of glowing things from red-hot to blue-white, and the white of daylight at the center; the marker reads any color's place. The third is what languages do with all this: eleven names, acquired in nearly the same order everywhere, dark and light first, red next, blue late.

The receptor curves are drawn as bells at the peaks Bowmaker and Dartnall measured in a human retina, 420, 534, 563 and 498 nm, with widths chosen to match the published sensitivities; they are not the measured curves point for point, and the lens and the yellow spot in front of the retina shift the effective peaks a little. The horseshoe is computed from the CIE 1931 two-degree observer in Wyman, Sloan and Shirley's analytic fit, between 420 and 640 nm, closed with the observer's tabulated points at 380, 660 and 700 nm where the fit's tails are unreliable; its fill is the nearest color a screen can show, so everything outside the triangle is shown less saturated than it is. The black-body curve is Planck's law run through the same observer. The naming sequence is Berlin and Kay's as revised by the World Color Survey; it is a strong tendency with exceptions, not a law.
