DNA

Two chains wound round each other, two nanometers across, ten and a half steps to a turn, and every step a pair of bases joined by hydrogen bonds: adenine with thymine, guanine with cytosine, always. That rule is the whole trick. Either chain carries the order of the other, so a cell can pull them apart and rebuild both, and the sequence of bases along one chain is a text.

The text is read three letters at a time, and each triplet names one of twenty amino acids or says stop; the second view shows the first thirty triplets of a real gene, the one for the beta-globin in blood, and lets any letter be changed to watch the protein change, including the single change that makes sickle cells. The third view is the whole text laid out by chromosome, three billion letters in one set.

The helix is drawn as B-DNA: a rise of 0.34 nm a base pair and 10.5 pairs a turn, so 3.6 nm a turn, where Watson and Crick's model had ten pairs and 3.4 nm; 2 nm across, the two backbones offset so the major groove is wider than the minor. The code is the standard table; the gene is the coding strand of HBB, read as its messenger RNA would be, with T standing in for U. The mutations are the classic ones at their real positions. Chromosome lengths are GRCh38's, gene counts are protein-coding genes as Ensembl tallies them and Wikipedia reports them, and both shift a little with each release; the gapless T2T sequence of 2022 comes to 3.055 billion base pairs.
