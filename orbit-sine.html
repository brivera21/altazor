Earth circles the Sun once a year, clockwise as seen from south of the ecliptic. With a time axis added, the circle becomes a helix: seen from the side, Earth bobs up and down, and its trace runs back through the years as a sine wave. The view turns with a drag, and a click on the trace takes the clock back to that moment.
Sources

The clockwise direction of both Earth's orbit and its spin is correct for a viewpoint south of the ecliptic, and the side-on projection of uniform circular motion really is a sine wave. The horizontal axis is time, not distance; Earth stays about 1 au (149.6 million km) from the Sun on a nearly circular path, drawn here as a circle run at an even pace.

The clock starts at the March equinox of 2026, 14:46 UT on March 20, and runs in tropical years of 365.2422 days. The equinoxes and solstices are marked at the times they fall, 92.74, 186.39 and 276.25 days after the March equinox, not at quarter turns: the orbit is slightly elliptical, Earth moves fastest in early January, and the northern spring and summer together last about seven and a half days longer than autumn and winter. Because the drawing runs at an even pace, those marks sit within two degrees of where the true ellipse would put them.

Sizes are drawn large so the Sun and Earth can be seen. To scale shrinks both to their true size against the orbit (the Sun's radius is 695,700 km, 0.47% of the orbit's; the Earth's 6,371 km, 0.004%), where the Earth is far less than a pixel and is kept as a point.
